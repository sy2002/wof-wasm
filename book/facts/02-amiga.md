# Fact sheet: chapter 2, The Amiga in twenty minutes

Every claim the chapter makes, one line each, with its source, in the order of the chapter. A claim without a source goes to the list at the end and stays out of the draft. A general fact about the Amiga or the 68000 that the repository's notes do not state is sourced in a reference work outside the repository, named with its chapter or section, and marked **(reference)**, so that the fact-check can weigh it; a fact the notes state about this game is always sourced in the notes. Two reference works are cited:

- *Amiga Hardware Reference Manual*, Commodore-Amiga, third edition (Addison-Wesley, 1991), cited as HRM with the chapter: 1 "Introduction" (the machine, its custom chips and their clocks), 2 "Coprocessor Hardware", 3 "Playfield Hardware", 5 "Audio Hardware", 6 "Blitter Hardware", 7 "System Control Hardware" (beam position detection, DMA, interrupts), 8 "Interface Hardware" (the game ports), Appendix F (the 8520 CIAs).
- *M68000 Family Programmer's Reference Manual*, Motorola (M68000PM/AD), cited as PRM with its section 1, "Introduction": the integer unit's programming model, its data formats and memory.

## Opening

1. The game's Amiga version asks for an Amiga 500, 1000 or 2000 with at least 512 KB of memory and a joystick. Source: `original/manual.txt`, page 2 (cited, not quoted).
2. The chapter covers only what the port needed, each hardware topic tied to what this game does with it; the deep dives are Part II. Source: `book/BOOK.md` 3, chapter 2 and Part II.

## The machine in one picture

3. The processor is Motorola's 68000, the chip that runs the program, one instruction after another. Source: PRM 1 and HRM 1 **(reference)**; `SPEC.md` 3.2 (the executable's 68000 code).
4. Around it sit the custom chips, the Amiga's own chips for the picture, the drawing and the sound, which, once told what to do, work without the processor. Source: HRM 1 **(reference)**; the game's use of them: `SPEC.md` 3.4, the two "custom chips" rows.
5. The custom chips are three: Agnus holds the copper and the blitter, Denise turns the picture's data into the signal for the screen, Paula plays the sound and also serves the disk drive, the serial port and the interrupts. Source: HRM 1 **(reference)**, the chips' descriptions; HRM 5 and 7 **(reference)** for Paula's audio and its interrupt registers; the name Paula for the sound side also in `SPEC.md` 6.5 and `re/notes/headless.md` ("Paula's audio side").
6. An interrupt is a signal from the hardware that makes the processor put aside what it is doing, run a short routine and carry on where it was. Source: the term's definition in the book's words; the game's use: `re/notes/headless.md`, "Scheduling" (the servers called as an interrupt calls them, on a stack of their own, the parked program's registers kept).
7. A register is a small named store inside a processor or a chip; a custom chip's registers sit at fixed addresses from `0xDFF000`, and a value written to one sets the chip to work. Source: HRM 1 **(reference)**; `re/notes/drawing.md`, "The blitter library" (`custom_base`, `0x026938`, holds `0xDFF000`).
8. Hexadecimal is base 16; this book marks it with `0x`. Source: the term's definition in the book's words; `book/BOOK.md` 4, point 3.
9. The game writes the registers directly: the copper's (`COP1LC` with copper lists of its own), the blitter's, the audio registers, `INTENA` and `INTREQ`. Source: `SPEC.md` 3.4, the custom-chip rows; `re/notes/drawing.md`, "The blitter library".
10. It reads few: the sound code reads the interrupt bits (`INTENAR`, `INTREQR`), the blitter's busy bit is read only to wait for it, and the two the port must answer for are `JOY1DAT` (the stick) and `VHPOSR` (the beam's position), served by the input layer and the entropy stream. Source: `re/notes/drawing.md`, "Read-back", the table's first two rows; `SPEC.md` 3.4, the custom-chip rows.
11. The figure `amiga-chips` (claims 3 to 5, 12 to 14, 32 to 38). Source: the figure, described under "Figures".
12. The CIAs are the Amiga's two interface chips, which serve its ports and carry timers; they sit at `0xBFD000` to `0xBFEFFF`; the game reads the fire button from CIA-A (bits 6 and 7 of its port register), and the music player takes CIA-A's timer A. The CIA is an 8520. Source: HRM Appendix F and 8 **(reference)** for their role; `re/notes/headless.md`, "Memory map"; `re/notes/input.md`, "Fire button and the tap/hold discrimination"; `re/notes/music.md`, "The timer" ("the 8520's reset state").
13. The heart of the operating system, AmigaOS, is called Kickstart: the Amiga 500 and 2000 carry it in their ROM, the memory that cannot be changed, and the Amiga 1000 loads it from a disk when it is switched on. Source: `original/manual.txt`, page 2 (the 1000 is started with its Kickstart disk); HRM 1 **(reference)** for the ROM of the 500 and 2000; `re/notes/system-font.md`, "The ROM" (the Kickstart 1.3 image of the A500 and A2000, with exec in it).
14. Kickstart holds among much else the fast floating point (`mathffp.library`), the key conversion (`console.device`) with the default keymap, and the system font. Source: `re/notes/headless.md`, "Game logic uses floating point" and "The keyboard needs the ROM too"; `re/notes/system-font.md`, "Where the fonts are".

## The 68000

15. The 68000 runs at about seven million cycles a second on a PAL Amiga: 7,093,790. Source: HRM 1 **(reference)**; ten times the E clock of `re/notes/music.md`, "The timer" (claim 91).
16. It has sixteen registers of 32 bits: eight data registers, D0 to D7, and eight address registers, A0 to A7, A7 marking the stack where routines keep their arguments and return addresses. Source: PRM 1 **(reference)**; `SPEC.md` 3.2 (arguments pushed on the stack, first at `8(a5)`); the listing shows `d1-d7/a0-a6` saved and `-(a7)` as the stack (`re/Wings.lst`, `text_width` `0x01591E`).
17. Memory is a long row of bytes, each with its address; an instruction moves a value between memory and a register, or computes with registers: adds, multiplies, compares or jumps. Source: PRM 1 **(reference)**.
18. An instruction works on a byte (8 bits), a word (16) or a long (32), written `.b`, `.w`, `.l` after its name. Source: PRM 1 **(reference)**; `SPEC.md` 7.1 (`muls.w` multiplies two 16-bit values into 32 bits, `ext.l` widens a 16-bit value).
19. Machine code is the program as the processor reads it, its instructions as bytes; assembly language is their written form, a short name for the operation followed by its operands. Source: the terms' definitions in the book's words.
20. The listing is written out by the disassembler, the tool that turns the bytes back into assembly language; chapter 4 tells how. Source: `SPEC.md` 2 (`re/Wings.lst` from `tools/disasm.py`); `book/BOOK.md` 3, chapter 4.
21. `rand_beam` (`0x0203BE` to `0x0203D8`, seven instructions, hand-written assembly) is the game's only random source in use, the one chapter 1 told of without naming it. Source: `re/Wings.lst` at the range; `re/functions.csv` (kind `asm`); `SPEC.md` 3.3; `re/notes/random.md`, "The generator" (`rand_lcg_unused` beside it, which nothing calls); chapter 1's fact sheet, claim 22.
22. Each instruction's line gives its address, its bytes of machine code, the instruction, and after a semicolon the name of a variable it touches; a `$` marks hexadecimal, as in Motorola's assemblers. Source: `re/Wings.lst` (e.g. `0x0203BE`); PRM **(reference)**, whose notation it is.
23. Line by line: the routine loads the word `rand_seed_const` (`0x026912`) into D0; multiplies it, signed, by `0x1AFB` into a 32-bit product (`muls.w`); adds `0x1FCCD` as a long; reads the word at `0xDFF006` into D1; exclusive-ors D1 into D0; stores the word into `rand_state` (`0x026910`); returns (`rts`). Source: `re/Wings.lst` `0x0203BE`-`0x0203D8`; `re/notes/random.md`, "The generator" (the same in C).
24. The result is left in D0 for the caller. Source: `SPEC.md` 3.2 ("Result in D0"); `re/notes/random.md`.
25. The fourth instruction reads `VHPOSR`, a custom-chip register at `0xDFF006`, which holds the beam's position: the line it is drawing, in its low eight bits (the register's high byte), and its place on that line (its low byte). Source: `re/notes/random.md`, "The generator" and the paragraph after it; HRM 7, "Beam position detection" **(reference)**.
26. The "seed" `rand_seed_const` is written only by `rand_set_seed`, which runs only when a demo is recorded or played back; so the multiply and the add always give the same number; `rand_state` is read only by `rand_lcg_unused`, which nothing calls. Source: `re/notes/random.md`, "The generator" and "The seed".
27. The beam alone is the chance, which is why chance in this game is a matter of timing. Source: `re/notes/random.md` ("All variation comes from the beam position"); `SPEC.md` 3.3.
28. The port answers that one read from its reproducible stream. Source: `SPEC.md` 7.3; `re/notes/random.md`, "Consequences".
29. `-$46ec(a4)` is a variable at an offset from the address in A4: `0x02AFFE` less `0x46EC` is `0x026912`, `rand_seed_const`; chapter 3 explains it. Source: `SPEC.md` 3.2 (A4 is `0x02AFFE`); computed; `book/BOOK.md` 3, chapter 3 ("the data reached through A4").
30. 223 routines were compiled from C, the game's own and those of its C library (`sprintf`, `format_float`, `create_port` among them), and look different in the listing; chapter 4 shows it. Source: `re/functions.csv` with `csv.DictReader`, kind C, 223 of 616; `SPEC.md` 3.2; `book/BOOK.md` 3, chapter 4.
31. The listing calls `rand_beam` from 42 places, 23 through one far-call slot and 19 through the other; the prose gives no count. Source: `grep -c '; -> rand_beam' re/Wings.lst` (42); `SPEC.md` 3.3 and `re/notes/random.md` say 43, which the controller corrects at the merge.

## Chip memory

32. Chip memory is the memory the custom chips can reach, reading and writing it by themselves, without the processor (direct memory access); memory a machine has beyond it, if any, only the processor reaches. Source: HRM 1 **(reference)**; HRM 7, DMA control **(reference)**.
33. So whatever a custom chip has to see must be in chip memory, and a program asks the operating system for it by a flag, as the game's `load_file_chip` (`0x01FECA`) does with `MEMF_CHIP`, `MEMF_PUBLIC` and `MEMF_CLEAR` (`0x10003`, the `pea` at `0x01FECE`). Source: `re/names.txt`, the line of `01feca`; `re/Wings.lst` `0x01FECA`-`0x01FEDE`.
34. Among what the game keeps there: its two screens' pictures (the bitplanes of both views, `0x159A0` bytes), the lists for the copper (three buffers of 1,000 bytes), a scratch buffer for the blitter (`MaskBuffer`, 1,040 bytes), and besides them the ticker's plane, a list for a black screen and the sprite pointers' block of zeroes. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "Memory" (`display_init` allocates everything from chip memory); `re/notes/drawing.md`, Summary.
35. Every shape container, a file holding many shapes, is loaded whole into chip memory. Source: `re/notes/shapes.md`, Summary and "Container and lookup" (`shapes_load` with `load_file_chip`).
36. The eight sound effects are loaded into chip memory. Source: `re/notes/sound.md`, "The samples".
37. The song data `wofsongs` has a DATA hunk of 39,020 bytes, the songs, the voices and the sound samples of the music, in chip memory. Source: `re/notes/music.md`, "The two files"; `SPEC.md` 3.1.
38. The table of pointers through which the game finds a shape by its number goes into ordinary (public) memory. Source: `re/notes/shapes.md`, Summary ("Game code then uses fixed numeric indices into these arrays") and "Container and lookup" (`shapes_resolve` allocates `4 * count` bytes of public memory).
39. The port has one static arena, from which the core hands out what the original asked the operating system for; the browser has no chip memory. Source: `SPEC.md` 3.4, the `AllocMem` row; `SPEC.md` 6.1 ("one static arena replaces `AllocMem`").

## Bitplanes

40. A picture is kept as bitplanes, one bit of every pixel's colour number to a plane; five planes for 32 colours, plane 1 the lowest bit, worth 1, to plane 5, worth 16; the display puts a pixel's bits together into a number from 0 to 31. Source: HRM 3 **(reference)**; for the shapes, `tools/ppkc.py`, `to_indexed` (each stored plane ORs its mask into the pixel); `re/notes/display.md`, "Screens" (`BPLCON0` `0x5200` for 5 planes, 32 colours).
41. The number goes through the palette, 32 colour registers (`COLOR00` upward) of 12-bit words, 4,096 colours. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, Summary and "The copper builder", step 4; chapter 1 introduced the palette.
42. `hc05` of `hellcat.shp`, the Hellcat in level flight, is 48 x 13 pixels in five planes; the marked pixel (x 22, y 8) has planes 1, 3 and 5 set, colour 21, `#4477AA` in `wingspalette`, the day mission's palette. Source: `tools/ppkc.py` `parse()` and `to_indexed()` of `hc05`; `book/tools/figures.py` `palette('wingspalette')`; `re/notes/display.md`, "Day and night" (`wingspalette` by day, `night.p` by night).
43. Its pixels use colours 19 to 31 only; every number from 16 upward has the 16 bit set, so plane 5 is the aircraft's whole silhouette. Source: `to_indexed()` of `hc05`; the figure `planes-hellcat`.
44. The figure `planes-hellcat`. Source: the figure, described under "Figures".
45. The playfield has 162 lines in low resolution, 320 pixels wide, with five planes and 32 colours; the port's picture is 640 wide and doubles each of these pixels. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, Summary; `SPEC.md` 6.4 ("The shell output is 640 pixels wide with low-resolution pixels doubled").
46. The dashboard has 37 lines in high resolution, 640 pixels wide, four planes, 16 colours; the ticker 13 lines, one plane; a blank line between each; 214 lines. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, Summary and "The play screen line by line".
47. The port keeps 8-bit indexed framebuffers, the colour number per pixel, and applies the palette at presentation; shapes are converted once, at load; the same numbers in every pixel are the second part of chapter 1's definition. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.4, first two paragraphs; `SPEC.md` 1, "Definition of faithful", point 2.

## The beam and the copper

48. The display draws the picture with a beam that sweeps each line from left to right and the lines from top to bottom. Source: HRM 3 and 7 **(reference)**; `re/notes/random.md` (`VHPOSR` holds the beam's line and place on the line).
49. A frame, one whole picture of the display, has 313 lines on PAL. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.5 ("a real PAL frame is 313 lines of 227 colour clocks"); `re/notes/porting-m8.md`, "What the model leaves out".
50. The game's picture begins at beam line 44 (`0x2C`), its line 0, and the play screen ends at beam line 257; above and below lie the border and the lines the beam spends on its way back to the top. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "The play screen line by line"; HRM 3 and 7 **(reference)** for the border and the vertical blank.
51. The copper is a small processor inside Agnus that follows a list of simple instructions in step with the beam; the game uses two kinds, a wait for a beam position and a move that writes a value into a chip's register (the third, a skip, it does not use). Source: HRM 1 and 2 **(reference)**; `re/notes/display.md`, "The copper builder" (`WAIT (0, y − 1)`, moves to `COLORxx`, `BPLCON0`), `cop_wait` (`0x019A9C`).
52. With it a program can change a colour or the screen mode at a chosen line of every frame, without the 68000. Source: HRM 2 **(reference)**; `re/notes/display.md`, "The copper builder" (moves to `COLORxx` and `BPLCON0` after waits).
53. The figure `beam-copper`. Source: the figure, described under "Figures".
54. The game writes its copper lists itself and points the copper at one through `COP1LC` (`0xDFF080`); the operating system's display routines are linked in and never called. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, Summary; `SPEC.md` 3.4, the graphics row of `InitVPort` and the others.
55. A list counts lines from the top of the picture, line 0 being beam line 44. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "The copper builder" (`cop_wait`: y + `0x2C`) and the viewport record (+0xA6, "line 0 is beam line `0x2C`").
56. Before line 0 the list sets the playfield's colours and planes. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "The copper builder", steps 4 and 5.
57. The split line is where the sky's palette gives way to the sea's; it is computed again in every pass; that it follows the horizon is inferred. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "The split line" and Summary.
58. There the list sets the sea's colours where they differ from the sky's, by day colours 2 to 15 and 24, fifteen. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "The copper builder", step 6, and "Day and night".
59. At the picture's lines 162 and 200 the list switches the planes off for a blank line and then sets up the dashboard and the ticker. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "The copper builder", step 2 ("This is what makes the line above each lower viewport blank").
60. On the ticker's first ten lines it sets `COLOR01` anew: 777, 999, BBB, DDD, FFF, DDD, BBB, 999, 777, 555, up to white and back down, ending darker than it began. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "How colours change", the ticker ramp row.
61. When the sky flashes, the game pokes one colour, `COLOR01`, into the built list. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "How colours change", the sky flash row.
62. A fade rebuilds and installs the list sixteen times, with no wait, so its length is processor time; chapter 7 takes it up. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "Fades"; `book/BOOK.md` 3, chapter 7.
63. The game keeps two complete screens, each with its own planes for the playfield and the dashboard, the ticker's one plane being shared, and each with its own copper list, which carries that screen's plane addresses. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, Summary ("The ticker is single-buffered and shared by both views"), "Records" (a view's copper list and bitplane memory) and "The copper builder", step 5 (the plane pointers).
64. A pass draws into the hidden screen; one write to `COP1LC` hands the copper the new list, which it takes up at the start of the next frame, and the screens swap with it. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "Double buffering and the swap" ("The hardware reloads the copper position from `COP1LC` at the next vertical blank").
65. Double buffering is drawing into a hidden picture and showing it only when it is whole. Source: the term's definition in the book's words; `re/notes/display.md`, "Double buffering and the swap" (the list installed after the drawing).
66. A third list is kept spare for the fades. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "Memory" (`cop_spare`, "a spare that fades swap in") and "Fades".
67. The port has no copper: it keeps a palette for every row, and the split, the ramp, the flash and the fades become further palettes. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.4 ("The per-row palette interface expresses all of these") and 6.6; `re/notes/porting-m1.md`, "Decisions the port made".
68. The figure `mission-palettes`: at VBlank 759 of the mission run, rows 0 to 150 through `wingspalette`, 151 to 161 through `ocean.palette` (the split at row 151, fifteen colours changed), 162 and 200 through the black palette 0, 163 to 199 through `iff-dash`, the ticker's rows through five palettes of one grey rising and falling and a sixth. Source: `book/docs/generated/figures/mission-palettes.png`, looked at; `book/figures.toml`; claim 58.

## The blitter

69. The blitter is a unit inside Agnus for copying and combining rectangles of memory, one bitplane at a time: up to three sources, A, B and C, combined bit by bit by a logic function chosen by a number, the minterm, into a destination, D; once started it works by itself and the processor is free. Source: HRM 1 and 6 **(reference)**; `re/notes/drawing.md`, "The blitter library" (one blit per plane) and "What `shape_draw` does, exactly" (A, B, C, minterm `0xCA`).
70. Every shape, rectangle and line of the play screen is drawn by one small set of hand-written assembly routines, at `0x0209BC` to `0x0215D8`, which program the blitter; the front end's dialogs draw with the operating system's `RectFill` and `Draw`. Source: `SPEC.md` 3.2; `re/notes/drawing.md`, Summary and "Text".
71. For a shape of two or more stored planes, the processor writes the OR of its planes into `MaskBuffer`, the mask; a shape of one plane is its own mask. Source: `re/notes/drawing.md`, "What `shape_draw` does, exactly", step 1.
72. The blitter runs once per plane, with the mask as A, the shape's plane as B and the screen's plane as C, and writes the shape's bit where the mask is set and keeps the screen's bit elsewhere; so colour 0 is see-through. Source: `re/notes/drawing.md`, "What `shape_draw` does, exactly", step 2 and "In hardware terms" (D = A·B + ¬A·C).
73. The figure `blit-hellcat`. Source: the figure, described under "Figures".
74. A shape whose plane is larger than the 1,040-byte buffer gets no mask and is drawn whole, colour 0 included; eleven shapes are that large. Source: `re/notes/drawing.md`, "What `shape_draw` does, exactly".
75. One is the rank badge of the briefing, `rank` in `world.shp`, drawn on an empty screen. Source: `re/notes/drawing.md` (`rank` "is drawn on an empty screen"); `re/notes/shapes.md`, "Names looked up at run time" (drawn centred on the briefing screen).
76. The other ten are the pieces of the carrier and the ships and contain no colour 0; where sky shows around a hull, the artwork carries the sky's colour 1: nine of the ten carry it, and `mcar`, the carrier's middle piece, has neither colour 0 nor colour 1. Source: `re/notes/drawing.md`, the same paragraph; `tools/ppkc.py` `to_indexed()` of the eleven shapes, colours 0 and 1 counted (the command in "Counts").
77. Nothing in the game's logic reads back what the blitter wrote: no pixel, no mask, no flag the blitter sets from its result (the zero flag); the one flag read, the busy flag, is only waited on. Source: `re/notes/drawing.md`, Summary and "Read-back".
78. In the headless original the copper and the blitter are taken out, and what the processor itself draws into memory, the masks and the text, still happens, unseen. Source: `re/notes/headless.md`, "What runs and what does not" (the custom-chip space plain memory) and "The stubs" (`BltTemplate` does nothing); `re/notes/drawing.md`, Summary (`shape_draw` and `text_render` write `MaskBuffer` by CPU).

## Paula

81. Paula is the sound chip: four channels, each given the address of a sound sample in chip memory, its length, a volume from 0 to 64 and a period. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.5; `re/notes/sound.md`, the slot table (volume 0 to 64); HRM 5 **(reference)**.
82. The period is how many cycles of the colour clock each byte is held; a smaller period plays higher. Source: `re/notes/sound.md`, "The slots and what they play" and the engine paragraph.
83. The colour clock is the clock the custom chips run on, 3,546,895 cycles a second on a PAL Amiga. Source: `re/notes/sound.md`, "The slots and what they play"; `SPEC.md` 6.5; HRM 1 **(reference)** for the chips running on it.
84. The channel plays the bytes by itself; at their end it starts again from the address in its register, and at each start it raises an interrupt, so that a program can hand it the next sound or stop it. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.5; `re/notes/headless.md`, "The audio channels".
85. Channels 0 and 3 go to the left, 1 and 2 to the right. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.5.
86. The eight sound effects are files of signed bytes, −128 to 127, without a header. Source: `re/notes/sound.md`, "The samples".
87. `sounds/boom` is 5,466 bytes, played once at period 500 by slot 4, the burst of a bomb or a rocket; a byte lasts about 0.14 ms, the whole 0.77 s. Source: `stat`; `re/notes/sound.md`, the slot table; computed 500 / 3,546,895 and 5,466 x 500 / 3,546,895.
88. The figure `sample-boom`. Source: the figure, described under "Figures".
89. The effects engine has eight slots, two per channel; the tick switches them and moves their volume and period; the first slot of a pair that is on gets the channel, so the aircraft's guns (slot 0, `machinegun`) drown its engine (slot 1, `Engine`) on channel 0. Source: `re/notes/sound.md`, "Two layers" and "The slots and what they play" ("the guns drown the engine").
90. The music is a separate small program, `songplay`, with its songs in `wofsongs`; the game loads both from the disk with `LoadSeg` when a song is to play and they are not loaded already (`music_stop` unloads them). Source: `re/notes/music.md`, "The two files" and "The game's calls"; `SPEC.md` 3.4, the `LoadSeg` row.
91. The music's beat comes from CIA-A's timer A, which counts the E clock, 709,379 times a second on PAL: a fifth of the colour clock, which is half the processor's 7,093,790 cycles. Source: `re/notes/music.md`, "The timer"; computed (709,379 x 5 = 3,546,895; x 2 = 7,093,790); HRM 1 **(reference)** for the clocks' relation.
92. Four of the five songs, 1 to 4, set the timer so that the music player's routine runs every 14,592 counts, 20.57 ms, a little longer than a PAL frame's 20 ms; song 0, the high-score screen's, every 15,616, 22.01 ms. Source: `re/notes/music.md`, "The timer" and "The game's calls" (song 0 on the high-score screen).
93. One byte of the timer is never written and is taken to keep its power-up value: the one assumption the music's tempo rests on; chapter 18 explains it. Source: `re/notes/music.md`, "The timer"; `SPEC.md` 6.5; `book/BOOK.md` 3, chapter 18; chapter 1's fact sheet, claim 94.
94. The port's Paula is the model the headless original runs; every sound sample started is logged on both sides with its channel, period and volume, and the logs must agree; the core mixes the channels. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.5, second paragraph, and 8, "Sound"; `re/notes/headless.md`, "The audio channels".

## The VBlank

95. The VBlank is the moment the beam goes back to the top and a new frame begins: 50 a second on PAL, 60 on NTSC. Source: the glossary entry; `SPEC.md` 6.2, Clock.
96. It reaches the program as an interrupt; the game has the operating system call two routines of its own at every one (exec's `AddIntServer`): the effects engine's `soundfx_vblank` first (priority 30), which starts the sounds the tick asked for, then `vblank_server` (priority −10), which counts the VBlanks, scrolls the ticker one pixel (the processor shifting the plane), and at every fourth VBlank takes an input byte into a queue. Source: `re/notes/headless.md`, "Scheduling"; `re/notes/sound.md`, "The channel layer"; `SPEC.md` 3.3 and 3.4; `re/notes/display.md`, "The play screen line by line".
97. The logic runs one logic tick for every input byte it takes from the queue, so the sampling at every fourth VBlank sets the pace: 12.5 ticks a second on PAL. Source: `SPEC.md` 3.3 (`run_queued_ticks`, one tick per queued byte).
98. A pass waits for the first VBlank since the previous pass handed over its copper list, unless one has gone by already; then it draws into the hidden screen, hands its list over, and runs a tick for each byte waiting. Source: `re/notes/display.md`, "Double buffering and the swap"; `SPEC.md` 3.3, the inner loop (`frame_update`, then `run_queued_ticks`).
99. On a real PAL Amiga a pass takes two VBlanks in a quiet scene, so two passes go to a tick. Source: `re/notes/passes.md`, "What the film of the real machine shows"; chapter 1's fact sheet, claim 35.
