7. Source: `web/index.html` lines 43-46 and their comment; `web/main.js`, `boot` (`decodeBase64` of `wof-wasm` and `wof-fs`).
10. Source: `web/core.js` lines 1-11; `SPEC.md` 5, step 4; chapter 22 (claim 14 of its sheet).
11. The wrapper asks the core for the geometry, puts the file blob into the core's arena, calls `wof_init` with a fixed seed, and takes its own buffers. Source: `web/core.js` lines 13-44; `web/main.js` (`SEED`); chapter 22's arena section.

The shell is a layer that knows no game: the clock, the picture in the Amiga's pixel aspect, the audio worklet, the raw Amiga key codes, the storage, the help screen, the pause sign and fullscreen would serve another game ported the same way. ## 2. The clock

14. The clock is a fixed-rate accumulator driven by `requestAnimationFrame`: it adds the real time between two animation frames and issues one `wof_vblank` for every fiftieth of a second of emulated time on PAL, every sixtieth on NTSC, each followed by one call of the pass entry. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, "Clock"; `web/clock.js` lines 1-7 and `frame` (lines 60-98).
15. **Animation frame** (the term, defined here): one call the browser makes of the page's `requestAnimationFrame` callback, before it repaints, at the display's refresh rate; none while the page is hidden. Source: the MDN references (requestAnimationFrame, Page Visibility). **(reference)**
16. **Emulated time** (the term, defined here): the game's own time, counted in VBlanks, 20 ms each on PAL, against the display's time, which the animation frames keep; "Emulated time, not display time, is what counts." Source: `web/clock.js` line 3; `web/audio.js` lines 3-5 ("the samples are emulated time"); chapter 7 ("What counts is the game's time, its VBlanks"). The 20 ms is derived (1/50 s), marked so.
17. **Accumulator** (the term, defined here): the store of real time not yet turned into VBlanks: every animation frame adds the time since the last, every VBlank issued takes a fiftieth of a second off, the rest waits for the next animation frame. Source: `web/clock.js`, `frame`; the "Fix Your Timestep!" reference for the technique's name. **(reference)** for the name.
18. Why: the original's inner loop runs one pass per displayed frame and the display is the VBlank, so the game's time is VBlanks, whatever the monitor: a 144 Hz monitor still gets 50 VBlanks a second on PAL, and a 30 Hz one 50 too. Source: `web/clock.js` lines 3-7; chapter 7 told the rule ("a monitor of 144 Hz and one of 30 Hz alike get 50 VBlanks a second"). The chapter links chapter 7 and adds the mechanism.
19. Listing 1, `frame` of `web/clock.js`: the next animation frame asked for first; the first frame only notes its time; a negative interval taken as 0; the accumulator capped at 24 VBlanks with the stall counted; the loop of `core.vblank(input.consume())` and `core.pass()` while a VBlank's worth remains; `video.present()` only if a VBlank was issued; `audio.pump()` every animation frame; the rates measured; the shell's other work (`onFrame`). Source: `web/clock.js` lines 60-98; `book/listings.toml` (the entry exists, shown by no chapter so far).
20. The frame's order after the VBlanks, in `onFrame`: the frame interval noted for the diagnostics overlay, the pause sign shown or hidden, the diagnostics overlay painted, the start of the sound checked, the flip remembered, the written files stored if they changed, the exit request checked. Source: `web/main.js` lines 142-150.
21. On a 60 Hz display on PAL five VBlanks fall in six animation frames, so one animation frame in six presents nothing and the picture changes 50 times a second; at 120 Hz seven animation frames in twelve present nothing. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, "The machine and the display's refresh". Figure (a) shows six animation frames and five VBlanks.
22. The figures box marked derived: a refresh at 60 Hz lasts 16.7 ms; a VBlank on PAL 20 ms; an animation frame counts as late from 1.5 refreshes, 25 ms at 60 Hz; at 120 Hz a refresh is 8.3 ms and late from 12.5 ms. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, "How it was measured" ("A frame longer than 1.5 refreshes (25 ms at 60 Hz)") and "The machine and the display's refresh" ("A frame there is 8.3 ms ... late from 12.5 ms on"); arithmetic of 1000/60 and 1000/50, marked derived.
23. After a stall, while the page is on view but the browser could not run it, at most 24 VBlanks are replayed: the original's own limit seen from outside, the input queue holding six input bytes of four VBlanks each, anything older dropped on the machine as well; the rest of the stall is dropped and the overlay counts the stall. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, "Clock"; `web/clock.js` lines 12-16 and 76-80 (`MAX_CATCHUP`, `stalls++`); chapter 7 told the rule. A page test holds that a stall is not made up frame for frame (`tests/test_page.py`, `test_a_stall_is_not_made_up_frame_for_frame`, chapter 24's).
24. The clock's rates for the diagnostics overlay: over windows of at least a second, VBlanks, ticks, calls of the pass entry and animation frames a second; the VBlanks per tick as the whole run's ratio, and whether the ticks are exactly a quarter of the VBlanks rounded up, because `vblank_server`'s divider starts at 0 and the first VBlank takes an input sample: N VBlanks give N / 4 input samples rounded up. Source: `web/clock.js`, `measure` (lines 33-58); `SPEC.md` 6.2, "Clock", last sentence; chapter 7 (the divider).
25. A change of the video standard sets the new period, empties the accumulator, starts the measurement window again, because a window across the change would report a rate that is neither, and tells the core (`wof_set_video_hz`). Source: `web/clock.js`, `setHz` (lines 118-126).
26. The clock starts at 50 Hz, PAL, the shell's default; the core starts at 60 and is told otherwise before the clock runs. Source: `web/clock.js` lines 19-21; `web/main.js` (`setStandard('pal')` before `clock.start()`).

## 3. The picture

27. The video standard is one shell setting, PAL or NTSC, which sets the VBlank rate and the pixel aspect together, as a machine has one or the other; the executable is the same for both and never checks; PAL is the default, because this disk comes from a PAL region, its added artwork is 256 lines high, and the owner's real Amiga is a PAL machine. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, "Video"; `web/video.js` lines 26-37; `web/main.js` lines 159-164. Chapter 1 gave the default's reasons: the chapter links it.
28. **Pixel aspect** (the term, defined here): how wide a pixel is shown against its height. On PAL a low-resolution pixel is 16/15 as wide as tall, so a framebuffer pixel, a high-resolution one, is 8/15, and the 640 x 214 framebuffer is shown in a box of 1024 : 642; on NTSC, where 320 x 200 fills a 4:3 screen, a low-resolution pixel is 5/6 and the box 800 : 642. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, "Video", **Aspect**; `web/video.js` lines 29-37 (`STANDARDS`); the Pixel aspect ratio reference for the term. **(reference)** for the term.
29. Never square framebuffer pixels, which would give a strip three times as wide as high. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2; `tests/test_page.py`, `test_the_picture_is_shown_in_the_display_aspect`'s docstring.
30. The size: the largest box of the ratio that fits the window, centred on black, worked out again on every resize, on entering and leaving fullscreen, and when `devicePixelRatio` changes, which happens without a resize when the window moves to a screen of another density or the page is zoomed; a media query on the current ratio is the only notice. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, **Size**; `web/video.js` lines 659-678; the devicePixelRatio reference. **(reference)** for the media query.
31. **Device pixel** (the term, defined here): one of the screen's own pixels, against a CSS pixel, the page's unit of layout; a Retina screen has two device pixels to a CSS pixel across. Source: the devicePixelRatio reference; `re/notes/page-video.md`, "The machine and the display's refresh" (`devicePixelRatio` 2). **(reference)**
32. The box is laid out in whole device pixels and only then converted to CSS pixels, so that the canvas's backing store is exactly its CSS size times `devicePixelRatio`, the picture lands on the screen's pixel grid and the browser resamples nothing a third time. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, **Size**; `web/video.js`, the comment above `fit`. "A third time": after the enlargement and the reduction (claim 34).
33. Listing 2, `fit` of `web/video.js`: the window in device pixels (`innerWidth * ratio`, rounded down); the box by the standard's ratio, width first, then height if it is too tall; the offsets to centre it; the canvas's backing store in device pixels and its style in CSS pixels; the pause sign and the help screen placed; the factors `kx` and `ky`, the smallest whole numbers whose enlargement is at least the box; the renderer resized and the last picture drawn again. Source: `web/video.js` lines 503-548.
34. **Two-step scaling** (the term, defined here): the picture enlarged by whole numbers with nearest neighbour, so that every framebuffer pixel becomes a block of exactly equal size, then reduced smoothly to the box, the one step that may fall between pixels, falling between blocks already large; where both factors are 1 the source is reduced directly. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, **Size**; `web/video.js` lines 9-13; the Image scaling reference. **(reference)** for nearest neighbour and bilinear.
35. Why two steps: the pixel aspect rules out whole-number factors in both directions; one smooth scaling would blur every pixel, one nearest scaling to a size between whole numbers would make some pixels a device pixel wider than others; two steps keep the pixels crisp and evenly sized. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2 ("Because the pixel aspect rules out whole-number factors in both directions, scaling is done in two steps so that pixels stay crisp and evenly sized"); `web/video.js` lines 9-13 ("so that every framebuffer pixel is a block of exactly equal size"); the Image scaling reference (nearest keeps edges, bilinear softens them). The "some pixels wider" clause is the meaning of "evenly sized", marked so.
36. The Canvas 2D way: three canvases, the 640 x 214 source that `putImageData` writes, the enlargement, and the canvas; the framebuffer is converted through the per-row palettes to RGBA first. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2; `web/video.js`, `create2dRenderer` and `convert`.
37. In Firefox the 2D canvas is software (Skia), so the reduction's `drawImage` onto the canvas blocks the main thread in proportion to the pixels it writes, about 3.6 ns a pixel; at the owner's Retina fullscreen, 3573 x 2240 device pixels, it took 28.65 ms, the whole `present()` 32.80, and the picture missed every other refresh, from about 3000 x 1900 device pixels up; Chrome draws its 2D canvases on the GPU and was never slow. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, "Summary" and "Before: the two-step 2D path"; `SPEC.md` 6.2; the Skia reference. **(reference)** for Skia.
38. The owner's screen: a Retina panel run at 1792 x 1120 CSS pixels at `devicePixelRatio` 2, so a fullscreen picture is 3573 x 2240 device pixels, the PAL box at the screen's full height; 60 Hz. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, "The machine and the display's refresh".

40. The WebGL renderer is the main way, the 2D renderer the fallback. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, "Video"; `re/notes/page-video.md`, "Summary".
41. **Renderer** (the term, defined here): the shell's code that puts the picture on the canvas: the WebGL renderer, the main one, or the 2D renderer, the fallback. Source: `web/video.js` lines 13-23 ("Two renderers do this"); `re/notes/page-video.md`.
42. WebGL 1 without extensions, which the browsers give wherever they give WebGL; nothing needs WebGL 2. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, "The WebGL path"; `SPEC.md` 6.2; the WebGL reference (OpenGL ES 2.0). **(reference)** for WebGL 1's base.
43. Three textures from the core's memory, a texture being an image the graphics processor reads: the framebuffer, 640 x 214, one byte a pixel (`LUMINANCE`), sent every present by one `texSubImage2D` straight from a view of the core's memory, no copy (137 KB); the palettes, 32 x 24 colours (`RGBA`), in the byte order an `ImageData` has; the row table, 214 x 1, each row's palette number; the last two compared with what was sent and sent again only when they differ, which a fade's step, the split line's move and the sky flash make them do; all read exactly, nearest, at texel centres. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, "The WebGL path", **Three textures**; `web/video.js` lines 204-262; `SPEC.md` 6.2. Texture glossed, not a term.
44. **Fragment shader** (the term, defined here): the small program the graphics processor runs once for every pixel it draws, to give it its colour. Source: the Shader reference; `re/notes/page-video.md` ("The fragment shader runs once per output pixel"). **(reference)**
45. One pass for both steps: the fragment shader runs once per device pixel of the canvas on one quad; it works out where the pixel's centre falls in the enlargement, 640 kx by 214 ky texels, as a smooth reduction samples it, and filters bilinearly from the four texels round that point; a texel of the enlargement is the framebuffer pixel whose block it lies in, the texel's index divided by the factor, rounded down, so the enlargement is never made: up to nine megapixels a frame at the owner's screen, never written. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, **One pass for both steps**; `web/video.js` lines 65-113 (`FRAGMENT`) and lines 14-20.
46. Where all four texels lie in one block, nearly every output pixel, there is nothing to blend and one lookup is made; at a block edge two or four. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`; `web/video.js`, the comment above `FRAGMENT`.
47. So the output is the two-step picture texel for texel and weight for weight, and the picture tests' tolerances hold unchanged. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, **One pass for both steps**; `SPEC.md` 6.2.
48. Colours are blended, never indices: each of the four texels is looked up through the palette of its own framebuffer row before anything is mixed, so across a row boundary each row keeps its palette; an interpolated index, or one palette for both, would put a colour between them that neither row has. The entry is the row's palette times 32 plus the pixel's number. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, **Colours are blended, never indices**; `web/video.js`, `colour` in `FRAGMENT`.
49. Listing 3, the shader's `colour` and `main` (`web/video.js` lines 84-113): the pixel's number and its row's palette number read from their textures, the palette entry, then in `main` the position in the enlargement, the two texels each way, the framebuffer pixels they belong to, and `mix` only where they differ. Source: `web/video.js` lines 84-113.
50. Precision: positions up to 3840 texels must be exact, so the shader asks for `highp` float, and the renderer takes the 2D way if the browser has none (fewer than 23 bits); every coordinate is a whole number in a float, and each division meant to land on a whole number gets half a unit to spare before `floor()`, against a graphics processor whose division comes out a hair low. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, **Precision**; `web/video.js` lines 144-148 and the comment above `FRAGMENT`.
51. The context: no alpha, no antialiasing, depth or stencil, the drawing buffer not kept (the compositor shows the last one on animation frames without a present); `failIfMajorPerformanceCaveat`, so a WebGL the browser runs only in software is refused: SwiftShader, Chrome's software WebGL, took 133 ms an animation frame at the owner's screen and 68 ms at 1280 x 900 at a scale factor of 2, four times the 2D renderer's own software time; no `powerPreference`, so a machine with two graphics processors keeps the integrated one. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, **The context**; `web/video.js` lines 134-142.
52. What `present()` costs on WebGL: one upload of 137 KB, two comparisons of 214 and 768 entries, one draw call; the shader's work is the graphics processor's. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, **What present() costs now**. (768 = 32 x 24, the palettes' entries.)
53. The 2D renderer is taken: when `getContext('webgl')` gives nothing (WebGL off or blocked, or only in software); when the shader does not compile or link, or `highp` is missing; when the context is lost and not given back within 2 s (`RESTORE_WAIT_MS`); and on `?video=2d`, which exists so that the tests and the owner can run the fallback on purpose, as `?audio=buffers` does for the sound. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, "The fallback, and the query"; `web/video.js` lines 51-53, 409-460; `SPEC.md` 6.2.
54. A canvas that has once given a WebGL context never gives a 2D one, so the fallback puts a new canvas in the old one's place, with the same id, class and style. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`; `web/video.js`, `replaceCanvas` and the comment above `createGlRenderer`.
55. On a lost context the shell calls `preventDefault()`, which tells the browser it wants the context back; restored, it makes the program and the textures again and draws the last picture; if the restored context cannot be set up, it falls back at once. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`; `web/video.js` lines 308-325, 438-451.

## 4. The sound

59. The core mixes Paula's channels VBlank by VBlank by emulated time (chapter 18); every animation frame, after the VBlanks it ran, the shell takes what was mixed through `wof_audio_render` and queues it behind what is playing. 60. **Audio frame** (the term, defined here): one instant of the mixed sound, a value for the left and one for the right; the core hands out as many as the emulated time lasts at the rate the shell asks for, the audio hardware's. Source: `web/audio.js` (`takeEmulated`: `core.renderAudio(core.audioFrames, rate)`; "frames of emulated time"); `web/core.js` (`renderAudio`: "interleaved stereo int16 at the given sample rate"); `SPEC.md` 6.1, `wof_audio_render`.
61. The shell takes the mixed sound in blocks of up to 8,192 audio frames, as many blocks as there are. Source: `web/core.js` line 36 (`audioFrames = 8192`); `web/audio.js`, `takeEmulated`.
62. Why a queue with bounds: the emulation's clock and the audio hardware's drift apart a little, so the queue is kept between two bounds: below 40 ms queued, silence tops it up to 100 ms; above 300 ms, the core's audio frames are dropped; both are counted on the diagnostics overlay. Source: `web/audio.js` lines 5-8 and 22-24 (`LOW_SECONDS`, `TARGET_SECONDS`, `HIGH_SECONDS`); `SPEC.md` 6.2, "Audio"; `re/notes/porting-m8.md`, **The page**.

64. Listing 4, `pumpWorklet` of `web/audio.js`: the queue as audio frames sent less audio frames played; every block taken, skipped while fresh, dropped and counted above the upper bound, otherwise posted; below the lower bound, silence up to the target, counted. Source: `web/audio.js` lines 198-222.
65. **Audio worklet** (the term, defined here): a small program the browser runs on its audio thread, beside the page; the shell's, `worklet.js`, takes the blocks the page posts and hands them to the audio hardware. 66. The worklet owns no state that matters: it plays the blocks in order, fills silence and counts an underrun when none is there, and reports every 16 of its calls how many audio frames it has played, by which the shell paces itself. Source: `web/worklet.js` lines 27-63.
67. The worklet's module cannot be a file next to the page, so it goes to the browser as a URL made on the spot: on a `file://` page a `data:` URL, because Chrome refuses a Blob URL there (the origin is opaque and the load counts as cross-origin), and elsewhere a Blob URL first. ## Where a source was wrong or silent

- **A. The restore's limit against the core's.** `web/core.js`'s `fsPut` refuses a file above 8,192 bytes (or a name above 62 characters), its scratch buffer 8,192 bytes, while the core's file system takes a written file up to `WOF_SAVE_MAX`, 12,412 bytes (`src/fs.c`, `FS_FILE_MAX`, whose comment gives a saved game as 4,258 bytes on map a and 11,516 on map m). `storeFiles` stores every written file; `restoreFiles` ignores `fsPut`'s answer, so a saved game above 8,192 bytes is stored, refused at the next page's start, and gone from the storage at that page's next store. The chapter states the restore as the code has it and claims no more; for the controller and the owner. (Chapter 17's "a reload keeps ... a saved game's memory" holds only below the shell's limit: for the controller.)
- **B. Stale comments in `web/`**, which the bundler keeps in the page's bytes; the chapter cites the code: `web/clock.js` lines 6-7 ("a 30 Hz one still gets 50, two per animation frame": on PAL one or two, five in three; two on NTSC); `web/input.js` line 14 ("samples a level 60 times a second": 50 on PAL) and line 15 ("for exactly one sample": until the next VBlank), line 18 ("the menus will need in M3"); `web/overlay.js` lines 1-3 ("the M0 acceptance criteria", "a steady emulated 60 Hz", "The backtick key toggles it": two codes) and the title it paints, "Wings of Fury - M4 diagnostics" (a string, not a comment); `web/worklet.js` lines 1-3 ("turns that string into a Blob URL, because a worklet module cannot be loaded from a file:// page any other way": a `data:` URL on a `file://` page); `web/core.js` line 151 ("M3 deliverable 7"); `web/main.js` lines 11-13 (the seed "belongs to M3"); `tools/build.py`, `inline_worklet`'s docstring ("handed to the browser as a Blob URL").
- **C. The pass after each VBlank.** The outline says "a pass after each" VBlank; chapters 7 and 22 call `wof_pass` the pass entry and keep "pass" for the game's round, two calls of the pass entry on PAL. The chapter says "a call of the pass entry".
- **D. The files restored "before the core starts".** The files are put back after `wof_init`, which runs the program to its first wait, and before the first VBlank (`web/main.js` line 115). The chapter says so.
- **E. The standard is not stored.** The outline asks whether the standard is a stored setting: it is not; the page starts on PAL every time (`web/main.js` line 164).
- **F. The dev keys reach the key buffer too.** `web/input.js`'s listener hands a digit to the core's key layer whatever the diagnostics overlay says, before `web/main.js`'s listener acts on it; nothing in the game takes a plain digit outside the line editor (not checked further). The chapter claims only that the development keys are offered while the diagnostics overlay is up.
- **G. `SPEC.md` 6.2's "the second fire key beside X".** The commit that made Space the one fire key, `60d3b7f`, shows the second fire key was `KeyZ`, the key beside X; the chapter says so.
