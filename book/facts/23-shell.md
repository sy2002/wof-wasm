Words used in the sheet as the chapter will use them, each in one sense. **An animation frame** is one call of the shell's `requestAnimationFrame` callback, at the display's refresh; **an audio frame** is one instant of mixed sound, a left and a right value; "frame" alone is not used. **The pass entry** is `wof_pass`, as chapters 7 and 22 call it; "a pass" alone stays the game's round of the inner loop, which on PAL takes two calls of the pass entry (chapter 7). **The box** is the picture's box on the page. **The canvas** is the displayed canvas. **A pixel** is named each time: a framebuffer pixel (640 by 214), a CSS pixel (the page's unit of layout) or a device pixel (the screen's own). **A renderer** is the shell's code that puts the picture on the canvas, the WebGL renderer or the 2D renderer; "path" is said only as the value `__wofVideo.path` carries. **A key** is a keyboard key; a name under which the browser stores a value is **a storage key**. **The pause** is the game's, asked for with `wof_request_pause`. **An absence** is the page's, hidden for a second or more; a page hidden for a few milliseconds is "a moment hidden". **The diagnostics overlay** is always named so; "the overlay" alone is chapter 22's overlay of the file system. **The help screen** and **the pause sign** keep chapter 1's names.

## 2. The clock

14. The clock is a fixed-rate accumulator driven by `requestAnimationFrame`: it adds the real time between two animation frames and issues one `wof_vblank` for every fiftieth of a second of emulated time on PAL, every sixtieth on NTSC, each followed by one call of the pass entry. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, "Clock"; `web/clock.js` lines 1-7 and `frame` (lines 60-98).
15. **Animation frame** (the term, defined here): one call the browser makes of the page's `requestAnimationFrame` callback, before it repaints, at the display's refresh rate; none while the page is hidden. Source: the MDN references (requestAnimationFrame, Page Visibility). **(reference)** Follow-up (B9): "in most browsers none while the page is hidden", the reference's "most browsers"; the shell stops its clock then in any case (`web/main.js`, `clock.stop()` in the visibilitychange handler).
16. **Emulated time** (the term, defined here): the game's own time, counted in the VBlanks the shell issues, against the display's time, which the animation frames keep; "Emulated time, not display time, is what counts." Source: `web/clock.js` line 3; `web/audio.js` lines 3-5 ("the samples are emulated time"); chapter 7 ("What counts is the game's time, its VBlanks"). The millisecond figures are the derived box's (claim 22), not the entry's (note 7).
17. **Accumulator** (the term, defined here): the store of real time not yet turned into VBlanks: every animation frame adds the time since the last, every VBlank issued takes a fiftieth of a second off, the rest waits for the next animation frame. Source: `web/clock.js`, `frame`; the "Fix Your Timestep!" reference for the technique's name. **(reference)** for the name.
18. Why: the original's inner loop runs one pass per displayed frame and the display is the VBlank, so the game's time is VBlanks, whatever the monitor: a 144 Hz monitor still gets 50 VBlanks a second on PAL, and a 30 Hz one 50 too. Source: `web/clock.js` lines 3-7; chapter 7 told the rule ("a monitor of 144 Hz and one of 30 Hz alike get 50 VBlanks a second"). The chapter links chapter 7 and adds the mechanism. Follow-up (C4): the two monitors are chapter 7's; the chapter keeps "whatever the display" in chapter 7's rule and gives no monitor; claim 137's clauses are out.
19. Listing 1, `frame` of `web/clock.js`: the next animation frame asked for first; the first frame only notes its time; a negative interval taken as 0; the accumulator capped at 24 VBlanks with the stall counted; the loop of `core.vblank(input.consume())` and `core.pass()` while a VBlank's worth remains; `video.present()` only if a VBlank was issued; `audio.pump()` every animation frame; the rates measured; the shell's other work (`onFrame`). Source: `web/clock.js` lines 60-98; `book/listings.toml` (the entry exists, shown by no chapter so far).
20. The frame's order after the VBlanks, in `onFrame`: the frame interval noted for the diagnostics overlay, the pause sign shown or hidden, the diagnostics overlay painted, the start of the sound checked, the flip remembered, the written files stored if they changed, the exit request checked. Source: `web/main.js` lines 142-150. Follow-up (B7, C6): in `frame()` the present, the sound's pump and the measurement come first (`web/clock.js` lines 90-94), then `onFrame`'s list; the chapter names the items plainly and gives the order's reason, the files stored before the exit request is read (claim 168).
21. On a 60 Hz display on PAL five VBlanks fall in six animation frames, so one animation frame in six presents nothing and the picture changes 50 times a second; at 120 Hz seven animation frames in twelve present nothing. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, "The machine and the display's refresh". Figure (a) shows six animation frames and five VBlanks.
22. The figures box marked derived: a refresh at 60 Hz lasts 16.7 ms; a VBlank on PAL 20 ms; an animation frame counts as late from 1.5 refreshes, 25 ms at 60 Hz; at 120 Hz a refresh is 8.3 ms and late from 12.5 ms. Source: `re/notes/page-video.md`, "How it was measured" ("A frame longer than 1.5 refreshes (25 ms at 60 Hz)") and "The machine and the display's refresh" ("A frame there is 8.3 ms ... late from 12.5 ms on"); arithmetic of 1000/60 and 1000/50, marked derived. Follow-up (C8): the box keeps the refresh and the VBlank; the late animation frame, 1.5 refreshes, 25 ms, stands in the sentence before the frame-time table.
23. After a stall, while the page is on view but the browser could not run it, at most 24 VBlanks are replayed: the original's own limit seen from outside, the input queue holding six input bytes of four VBlanks each, anything older dropped on the machine as well; the rest of the stall is dropped and the overlay counts the stall. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, "Clock"; `web/clock.js` lines 12-16 and 76-80 (`MAX_CATCHUP`, `stalls++`); chapter 7 told the rule. A page test holds that a stall is not made up frame for frame (`tests/test_page.py`, `test_a_stall_is_not_made_up_frame_for_frame`, chapter 24's). Follow-up (B4, C5): the stall for the player: a while in which the page is on view but the browser does not run it (chapter 7's words); the game jumps ahead by at most 24 VBlanks and the rest of that time never happens for it (`SPEC.md` 6.2, "Clock"); `stalls++` counts the stall, not the time lost: "the stall is counted". No example of a stall's cause is given: no source names one.
24. The clock's rates for the diagnostics overlay: over windows of at least a second, VBlanks, ticks, calls of the pass entry and animation frames a second; the VBlanks per tick as the whole run's ratio, and whether the ticks are exactly a quarter of the VBlanks rounded up, because `vblank_server`'s divider starts at 0 and the first VBlank takes an input sample: N VBlanks give N / 4 input samples rounded up. ## 3. The picture

27. The video standard is one shell setting, PAL or NTSC, which sets the VBlank rate and the pixel aspect together, as a machine has one or the other; the executable is the same for both and never checks; PAL is the default, because this disk comes from a PAL region, its added artwork is 256 lines high, and the owner's real Amiga is a PAL machine. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, "Video"; `web/video.js` lines 26-37; `web/main.js` lines 159-164. Chapter 1 gave the default's reasons: the chapter links it.
28. **Pixel aspect** (the term, defined here): how wide a pixel is shown against its height. On PAL a low-resolution pixel is 16/15 as wide as tall, so a framebuffer pixel, a high-resolution one, is 8/15, and the 640 x 214 framebuffer is shown in a box of 1024 : 642; on NTSC, where 320 x 200 fills a 4:3 screen, a low-resolution pixel is 5/6 and the box 800 : 642. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, "Video", **Aspect**; `web/video.js` lines 29-37 (`STANDARDS`); the Pixel aspect ratio reference for the term. **(reference)** for the term. Follow-up (C9): the numbers in a small table (standard, a low-resolution pixel's width to height, the box); 16/15 is the machine's proportion as `SPEC.md` 6.2 states it, no further source; NTSC's 5/6 follows from 320 by 200 filling a 4 : 3 screen (`SPEC.md` 6.2), each row told the same way.
29. Never square framebuffer pixels, which would give a strip three times as wide as high. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2; `tests/test_page.py`, `test_the_picture_is_shown_in_the_display_aspect`'s docstring.
30. The size: the largest box of the ratio that fits the window, centred on black, worked out again on every resize, on entering and leaving fullscreen, and when `devicePixelRatio` changes, which happens without a resize when the window moves to a screen of another density or the page is zoomed; a media query on the current ratio is the only notice. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, **Size**; `web/video.js` lines 659-678; the devicePixelRatio reference. **(reference)** for the media query.
31. **Device pixel** (the term, defined here): one of the screen's own pixels, against a CSS pixel, the page's unit of layout; a Retina screen has two device pixels to a CSS pixel across. Source: the devicePixelRatio reference; `re/notes/page-video.md`, "The machine and the display's refresh" (`devicePixelRatio` 2). **(reference)**
32. The box is laid out in whole device pixels and only then converted to CSS pixels, so that the canvas's backing store is exactly its CSS size times `devicePixelRatio`, the picture lands on the screen's pixel grid and the browser resamples nothing a third time. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, **Size**; `web/video.js`, the comment above `fit`. "A third time": after the enlargement and the reduction (claim 34).

34. **Two-step scaling** (the term, defined here): the picture enlarged by whole numbers with nearest neighbour, so that every framebuffer pixel becomes a block of exactly equal size, then reduced smoothly to the box, the one step that may fall between pixels (`web/video.js` line 12, "may fall"), falling between blocks already large; where both factors are 1 the source is reduced directly. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2, **Size**; `web/video.js` lines 9-13; the Image scaling reference. **(reference)** for nearest neighbour and bilinear. Follow-up (B8, C11): "may fall"; every framebuffer pixel becomes exactly `kx` by `ky` pixels of the enlargement (`web/video.js` lines 9-13: "so that every framebuffer pixel is a block of exactly equal size").
35. Why two steps: the pixel aspect rules out whole-number factors in both directions; one smooth scaling would blur every pixel, one nearest scaling to a size between whole numbers would make some pixels a device pixel wider than others; two steps keep the pixels crisp and evenly sized. Source: `SPEC.md` 6.2 ("Because the pixel aspect rules out whole-number factors in both directions, scaling is done in two steps so that pixels stay crisp and evenly sized"); `web/video.js` lines 9-13 ("so that every framebuffer pixel is a block of exactly equal size"); the Image scaling reference (nearest keeps edges, bilinear softens them). The "some pixels wider" clause is the meaning of "evenly sized", marked so.
