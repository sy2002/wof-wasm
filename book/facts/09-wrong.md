## 1. The stick's bits

5. The listing gives the stick's decoder, `read_joy_bits` (`0x01520E`), as a few instructions and a table: it reads `JOY1DAT`, builds a four-bit index from the register's bits 9, 8, 1 and 0, looks the index up in `joy_bits_table` (`0x0256A6`, 16 bytes), and swaps bits 0 and 1 when `opt_invert_vertical` is set and a vertical bit is set. Source: `re/Wings.lst` `0x01520E`–`0x015248` (`move.w $dff00c.l, d0`; `lsr.w #$6, d1`; `and.w #$3, d0`; `and.w #$c, d1`; `or.w d1, d0`; `lea.l -$5958(a4), a0 ; joy_bits_table`; `move.b (a0, d0.w), d0`; `eori.w #$3, d0` behind `tst.b ... ; opt_invert_vertical`); `re/notes/input.md`, "Direction decoding".
6. The listing shown: `read_joy_bits` as `asm` with its header, 21 lines (claim 98).
7. What was believed: bit 0 of the input byte is the stick pulled back ("down") and bit 1 the stick pushed forward ("up"); the specification's sections 3.3 and 6.1 and `re/notes/input.md` said so, and the first shell mapped the up key to bit 1. Source: commit `b5f4335` (message: "Sections 3.3 and 6.1 and re/notes/input.md had the two the wrong way round; the shell and the harness's letters still have"; its diff of `SPEC.md` 3.3, "bit 0 stick down, bit 1 stick up", and of 6.1, "bit 0 down, bit 1 up"); commit `e1614f4` ("The up key is the stick pushed forward, bit 0": `web/input.js` changed).
8. Why it was reasonable: the reading took the table for the standard decode of the Amiga's joystick, which it is, and wrote that rule down with the vertical pairs exchanged ("`up = b1 xor b0`, `down = b9 xor b8`"); nothing inside the program can catch such a slip, because the table is consistent either way: which pair of the register's bits is the forward switch is a fact of the hardware, not of the code. Source: commit `b5f4335`, its diff of `re/notes/input.md` (the old line "That is the standard Amiga gray-code decode (`right = b1`, `left = b9`, `up = b1 xor b0`, `down = b9 xor b8`) baked into a table"; the new "`back = b1 xor b0`, `forward = b9 xor b8`"); `SPEC.md` 3.3 ("Forward ... which the hardware reports as bit 9 exclusive-or bit 8 of `JOY1DAT`"). "Consistent either way" is reasoning from the table: the same sixteen bytes serve either naming, with only the names of bits 0 and 1 exchanged.
9. The harness did not trust the reading: from its first version it built its table of register values by running the original's own decoder on all sixteen combinations of the four direction bits and keeping, for each answer, the value that gave it. Source: `tools/headless.py`, `_joy_words` ("JOY1DAT for each stick position, found by asking the original's own decoder"); `git log -S"_joy_words" -- tools/headless.py` (first in `96c8008`, "The headless original: the game's own code under Unicorn"); `re/notes/headless.md`, "Input".
10. What the instrument showed: the headless original's take-off lifted off only with bit 0, the bit the reading called "down", and rolled over the bow without it or with bit 1; asked with `JOY1DAT` `0x0100`, the forward switch alone, the decoder returns 1, which is bit 0, and with `0x0001`, the back switch alone, 2. Source: commit `eed2748`, its diff of `re/notes/headless.md` (the old text: "Bit 0 is the stick pushed forward, not pulled back ... Asked with `JOY1DAT` = `0x0100`, forward alone, `read_joy_bits` returns 1 ... In flight bit 0 climbs: the take-off below needs it, and with bit 1 instead the aircraft goes over the bow"; the old script "stick right together with bit 0 (`RD`) after 460 VBlanks of rolling lifts it off"); `re/notes/headless.md`, "Input" as it stands.
11. The menus agree: for the forward switch the menus' own decoder `read_joy_dir8` (`0x020488`) returns 1, which `menu_input` answers with −1, exactly as it answers the cursor-up key `0x4C`; the back switch gives 5 and +1, like cursor-down. Source: `re/notes/headless.md`, "Input" ("The menus treat forward as up ..."); `re/notes/input.md`, "Direction decoding"; `tests/test_headless.py`, `test_bit_0_of_the_input_byte_is_the_forward_switch` (`answers == {'forward': (1, 1), 'back': (2, 5), ...}`).
12. The manual's take-off instructions say the same, and the owner's real Amiga agrees: pushed forward, the stick climbs. Source: `SPEC.md` 3.3 ("the manual's take-off instructions say the same and the owner's real Amiga confirms it"); `original/manual.txt`, page 5 (the take-off: the stick pushed up to give elevation; cited by page, not quoted); `CONTROLLER.md`, "Open items" ("confirmed by the user on the real Amiga"). The order (the Amiga after the harness) is not stated in the repository; the chapter says "agrees", in the present.
13. What was wrong: the reading's names, not the game: bit 0 is the stick pushed forward, which is up in the menus and climbs in flight. Source: `SPEC.md` 3.3 ("bit 0 stick forward, bit 1 stick back ... it moves the cursor up in the menus and **climbs** in flight"); `re/notes/input.md`, "Input byte layout".
14. What changed: the specification and the input note turned round; the shell's up key and a gamepad pushed forward give bit 0; the harness's letters mean what they say, `U` bit 0 and `D` bit 1; two tests pin the facts by running the original; a page test in both browsers holds each key through the driver and watches what the core is handed. Source: commits `b5f4335` (the specification, the note), `e1614f4` (`web/input.js`, `web/overlay.js`), `eed2748` (`tools/headless.py` `RAW_BITS`, `tests/test_headless.py`), `a36eae6` (message: "a page test in both browsers holds each key through the driver and watches what the core is handed"); `tests/test_headless.py`, `test_bit_0_of_the_input_byte_is_the_forward_switch` and `test_pushing_forward_climbs_and_pulling_back_does_not`.
15. The rule that followed: no reading of the stick's bits is assumed; the bits are what the running original answers, and the tests hold them there. The lesson the project drew: the reading mistake was found by an observation, not by a better reader. Source: `re/notes/headless.md`, "Input" ("Two tests pin these facts"); `CONTROLLER.md`, "Models and effort" ("A reading mistake made earlier in this project (the stick's up and down bits) was found by observation, not by a better reader"); `book/facts/06-headless.md`, claim 51. ## 2. The shift that lost its sign

17. What was believed: the oracle, Unicorn set to be a 68000, runs the game's code as the chip does. Why reasonable: the oracle is built on a widely used emulator's processor core and passes its own self-test, the original's unpacker on all ten packed files against the project's decoder. Source: `book/docs/part-1/oracle.md`, "The arrangement" (Unicorn, built on QEMU) and "The instrument is checked too" (the self-test); `tools/oracle.py` (run on its own, its self-test); chronicle's wording "had been trusted" is not cited.
18. What the instrument showed: the oracle test of `objects_step` over random states failed although the port was right: the original under the emulator bounced a bomb on an airfield's runway into a vertical speed of +9 where a 68000 gives −2. Source: `re/notes/porting-m5.md`, "The emulator's memory-form shift (observed)"; `tests/test_oracle_m5.py`, `test_the_objects_step_matches_the_original`; `book/docs/part-1/oracle.md`, "The instrument is checked too"; chapter 5's sheet, claim 48.
19. What was wrong: the emulator decides whether a shift of a word in memory is arithmetic or logical from bit 3 of the opcode, which in that form belongs to the address mode, so `asr.w d16(An)` shifts logically (`0xFFFD` to `0x7FFE`). Source: `re/notes/porting-m5.md`, the same section; `re/notes/headless.md`, "Unicorn, as it behaves here", last point; `CONTROLLER.md`, "Pitfalls that cost time" (Unicorn 2.1.4).
20. What changed: a hook corrects the executable's three `asr.w d16(An)` in the oracle and so in the headless original, which runs on it; every shift and rotate of a memory word in the program was surveyed, and no other one's result is affected; a test holds the fault, the correction with its flags, and the list against the listing; any new instrument built on the emulator must install the same hooks. Source: `tools/m68k_fix.py`, `ARITHMETIC_SHIFTS` (`0x010BB8`, `0x010BE0`, `0x019D5E`: three entries); `re/notes/porting-m5.md`, the same section (the `lsr.w`, the two `asl.w`, the 42 `roxl.w`); `tests/test_headless.py` (`_shift_once` and the tests after it); `CONTROLLER.md`, "Pitfalls that cost time" ("Any new instrument built on Unicorn must install the same hooks"); commit `7e2bd7d`.
21. No mission script reached the bounce: no recorded mission met the fault, a test over random states did. Source: `re/notes/porting-m5.md`, the same section ("No script reaches the bounce"); `CONTROLLER.md`, "Pitfalls that cost time" ("Found at M5 by an oracle test over random states, not by any script").
22. The lesson: the instrument is checked too; a test that fails a right port is a finding about the instrument. Chapter 5 tells the mechanism with its figure `shift-bits.svg` and the hook's listing; this chapter refers to them and draws neither again. Source: `book/BOOK.md` 3, chapter 5 ("the instrument is checked too"); `book/docs/part-1/oracle.md`, "The instrument is checked too".
23. Instrument named: the oracle (chapter 5). Source: `book/BOOK.md` 3.

## 3. Two runs that differed: the timeout

24. What was believed: two runs of one run description give the same dump, byte for byte; that was the headless original's acceptance and a test holds it. Source: `SPEC.md` 9, the row M2 ("two runs give identical dumps"); `tests/test_headless.py`, `test_two_runs_give_identical_dumps` and, slow, `test_a_long_flight_twice`; `book/docs/part-1/headless.md`, "The same run, twice".
25. Why reasonable: every check of it had passed, and the harness's one use of the clock, a timeout that ended each slice of emulation after two seconds, could fire only if a slice took that long, which on a quiet machine it never does: a slice normally reaches the next wait within milliseconds. Source: `re/notes/headless.md`, "Unicorn, as it behaves here" ("With the old slices of two seconds this needs the process to stall that long, which the full suite once met"); `book/docs/part-1/headless.md`, "The wall clock never shapes a run" ("A slice normally reaches the next wait within milliseconds"); chapter 6's sheet, claim 91.
26. What the instrument showed: a run of the full suite on a loaded machine failed both loops of one M6 script, `japcarrier_m`, once, and the same tests passed when run alone; the original's two recordings of that script differed from tick 692 on, the oil timer one apart, 43 against 44, with identical inputs and entropy. Source: `CONTROLLER.md`, "Pitfalls that cost time" ("A loaded machine met the first in the full suite ... (`japcarrier_m`, the oil timer one apart at tick 692, the two M6 loops failing and passing alone)"); `CONTROLLER.md`, "The plan ahead" ("whose two recordings of the script differed from that tick on with identical inputs and entropy"); `re/notes/headless.md`, "Unicorn, as it behaves here" ("the oil timer's `subq.w #1` at `0x0113A2` ran twice, 43 against 44 from tick 692 on"); commit `d7394ae` (message: "The same mission script recorded twice could differ (`japcarrier_m` in the full suite, the oil timer one apart from tick 692 on)").
27. The oil timer, glossed as chapter 6 glosses it: the timer by which a damaged aircraft loses its oil. Source: chapter 6's sheet, claim 91 (`logic_tick` takes one oil every `0x50` ticks once a target's gun has hit the aircraft; `re/notes/porting-m5.md`).
28. The reasoning that pointed at the instrument: with the inputs and the stream of chance the same, the program's own determinism (chapter 6) leaves the harness as the only place a difference can come from. Reasoning from claim 26 and from `book/docs/part-1/headless.md`, "The same run, twice" ("Nothing the program can see depends on a clock"). The chapter's own voice.
29. How the mechanism was found and measured: the rare fault was made frequent by shortening the slices to three thousandths of a second, about 21,000 timeouts a recording; ten such recordings of `japcarrier_m` all differed from the unhurried one, from steps 17 to 185 on, always a counter one apart at first; one crashed on a corrupted return address and three hung. Source: `re/notes/headless.md`, "Unicorn, as it behaves here", the second point ("both measured on `japcarrier_m` with slices of 3 ms, about 21,000 timeouts a recording (Unicorn 2.1.4). Ten such recordings all differed from the unhurried one, from steps 17 to 185 on, always a counter one apart at first; one crashed on a corrupted return address and three hung"); commit `d7394ae` ("With slices of 3 ms, every one of ten recordings differed, one crashed and three hung"). Chapter 6 gives the ten, the one and the three; the chapter refers to them and keeps "made frequent" as the point.
30. The measured account (sheet only, or a clause): of 9,799 timeouts in one recording, 4,594 stopped on the instruction of their slice's last memory-hook event, and 4,588 of those accesses were made a second time by the next slice: 179 writes of a different value, an increment to memory counted twice, 434 beam reads that took a second entropy value. Source: `re/notes/headless.md`, "Unicorn, as it behaves here", the first sub-point.
31. What was wrong, in one sentence (chapter 6 tells it whole): a timeout stops the emulation from a timer beside it, wherever the program is; landing while a memory hook runs, it makes the next slice run an instruction a second time, and landing in the middle of the emulator's own stopping, it silences every hook so that the run spins for ever. Source: `re/notes/headless.md`, "Unicorn, as it behaves here"; `book/docs/part-1/headless.md`, "The wall clock never shapes a run"; commit `d7394ae`.
32. What changed: slices end by a count of instructions, at a place the program alone decides; the wall clock only ends a run, as Stuck; a test runs one flight in slices of three lengths and demands the same records. Rule: the wall clock never shapes a run, and no run that goes on afterwards is given a timeout. Source: commit `d7394ae`; `re/notes/headless.md`, "Unicorn, as it behaves here" ("Wall time only ever ends a run, as `Stuck`, and never shapes one"; "Never end a slice by `emu_start`'s `timeout` in a run that goes on afterwards"); `tests/test_headless.py`, `test_where_a_slice_ends_changes_nothing`, `test_wall_time_only_ever_ends_a_run_as_stuck`; `CONTROLLER.md`, "Pitfalls that cost time" ("Never give `emu_start` a timeout in a run that goes on afterwards").
33. The lesson: a failure that goes away when a test is run alone is not noise; with inputs fixed it is a fault in the instrument, and the way to study a rare fault is to make it frequent. Reasoning from claims 26 to 29; `CONTROLLER.md`, "Pitfalls that cost time" ("Run the suite again on a quieter machine before believing such a failure", for the page tests) is the opposite case and is not cited. The chapter's own voice.
34. Instruments named: the full suite (chapter 24), the comparison of two recordings (chapter 6's determinism). Source: `book/BOOK.md` 3.

## 5. The register carried as a long

49. A 68000 register holds 32 bits; an instruction on a word works on its lower 16 bits and leaves the upper word as it was, so a value a routine leaves in a register as a long reaches the next routine's upper word. Source: `SPEC.md` 7.1 ("A register a routine leaves as a long reaches the next routine's upper word"); the glossary's 68000 entry (bytes, words of 16 bits, longs of 32). **(reference)** for "an instruction on a word leaves a data register's upper word as it was": Motorola, *M68000 Family Programmer's Reference Manual*, section 1, on the data registers and the organization of data in them (an operand of a word uses and changes the register's low-order 16 bits and neither uses nor changes the rest; an address register is the exception, a word written to it is sign-extended), the Internet Archive copy the glossary already links. D1, the register of story 5, is a data register.
50. Upper word, a term of this chapter: the upper 16 bits of a 32-bit register or long. Source: claim 49.
51. What was believed: the targets' walks are word-sized throughout; the upper words of D0 and D1 at the targets' routines are 0. Why reasonable: they were 0 at every entry over two scripts, observed with observers on the entries. Source: `re/notes/porting-m5.md`, "Registers that cross a call" ("the upper words of D0 and D1 at the targets' routines were 0 at every entry over `rockets_c` and `guns_a`, observed with observers on the entries"); the chronicle's wording "treated the targets' walks as word-sized throughout" is not cited.
